To find every recurring charge on a bank statement, look for the same merchant appearing at a regular interval: every week, month, quarter or year. Use 12 months of statements, not one, so annual renewals show up. Then check three things for each charge: what it really is, whether the amount changed, and whether you still want it.

The obvious ones take a minute. The work is in the rest: charges billed under a name you don't recognize, small amounts your eye skips, trials that quietly became paid plans, and charges that kept coming after you cancelled. This guide covers each of them.

It works the same for a business card, a personal card that pays for business tools, or a household account.

## How do I find recurring charges on a bank statement, step by step?

:::steps
1. **Download 12 months of statements.** CSV is easiest to sort; PDF works if that's all your bank offers. Include every card and account that pays for anything regular.
2. **Sort by merchant.** Charges from the same merchant line up, and anything that repeats becomes visible in seconds.
3. **Mark every merchant that appears more than once.** Note the amount, the interval and the day of the month it lands.
4. **Look for single charges that might be yearly.** Software, domains, insurance and memberships often charge once a year. A lone $99 or $299 charge from a software company is worth a closer look.
5. **Work out the names you don't recognize.** Search the merchant text, then search your inbox for the amount.
6. **Compare amounts across months.** Any change in a fixed-price charge is a price rise, an extra seat or a plan change.
7. **Put everything on one list** with its monthly and yearly cost, so you can decide what to keep.
:::

:::tip[Sort twice]
Sort by merchant to find what repeats, then by amount to find what matters. The largest recurring charges deserve the first look. The smallest deserve one too, because nobody else will give them one.
:::

## Why doesn't the merchant name match the product?

The name on your statement is whatever the seller's payment setup sends to your bank, and it often isn't the product you signed up for. The usual reasons:

- **Payment processors and resellers.** Some software companies sell through a reseller, so a name like Paddle or FastSpring can appear next to the product, or instead of it.
- **Parent companies.** The charge carries the legal company name, not the brand. A tool you know by its app name may bill as a company you've never heard of.
- **App stores.** Subscriptions bought inside an iPhone or Android app are billed by Apple or Google under their own name, and several purchases can be grouped into one charge. To see what each covers, open the subscriptions list in your Apple or Google Play account.
- **Shortened text.** Banks cut descriptions short and add reference codes, so even a familiar name can look strange.

To identify a charge, search the exact merchant text, then search your email for the amount and the date. The receipt is usually there.

## Is a charge monthly, annual or usage-based?

Not every recurring charge looks the same. Knowing the pattern tells you where to look and what to watch.

| Type | How it shows up | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly | Same merchant, same amount, around the same day | Price rises and extra seats |
| Annual | One large charge a year | The renewal date, a few weeks ahead |
| Quarterly or weekly | Same amount on a longer or shorter rhythm | Totals that add up faster than they look |
| Usage-based | Same merchant every month, amount changes | A jump in usage you didn't plan |
| Per seat | Amount steps up as people are added | Seats for people who left |

Usage-based charges, like cloud hosting, text messaging or AI API bills, are recurring even though the amount moves. Treat each one as a single item with a typical monthly range, and look closer when a month lands well outside it.

Charges in another currency can also shift from month to month with the exchange rate, and some cards add a foreign transaction fee on top. A small change on a charge billed in euros or pounds may be the rate, not a price rise.

## Which small charges are easy to miss?

The eye skips anything under $10, and those are often the charges that run for years: extra cloud storage at $2.99, an app you tried once, a $1 add-on, a domain that renews every year for around $15.

One at a time they're nothing. Together they add up: ten small charges averaging $6 a month is $720 a year. Mark every small repeating charge and ask it the same question you'd ask a big one: would I sign up for this today?

## How do I spot a trial that turned into a paid plan?

Look for a merchant whose first real charge appears out of nowhere, often a week or a month after a tiny charge or a sign-up from the same company. Free trials that ask for a card usually convert to a paid plan automatically when they end, and the first charge can be the full monthly or yearly price.

Search your email for "trial", "welcome" or the company name to find when you signed up. If you didn't mean to keep it, cancel now and ask the vendor whether they'll refund the first charge. Some will if you ask promptly.

## How do I catch price rises between months?

Compare each fixed-price charge with the same charge a few months earlier. Increases are often small enough to miss: a plan going from $29 to $35 a month is a rise of about 21%, or $72 more a year.

Rises come from three places: the vendor changing its prices, your plan moving up a tier, or someone adding seats. Each needs a different response. A price change is a good moment to ask whether the tool is still worth it. A tier change may be a mistake. An added seat should match a person who joined.

## What if a charge continues after I cancelled?

A charge after cancelling is one of the clearest signals on a statement, and it's worth acting on quickly. Work through it in this order:

1. **Find your proof.** The cancellation confirmation email, a screenshot of the account page, or at least the date you cancelled. Keep it somewhere you can find again.
2. **Check where you cancelled.** The charge may come from a second workspace, a different account, or an app store subscription you cancelled in the wrong place.
3. **Contact the vendor.** Send the date you cancelled, your proof and the charge you want refunded, and ask for written confirmation that the subscription is closed. Many vendors fix this once they see proof.
4. **Dispute with your card issuer as a last resort.** If the vendor doesn't respond or refuses, contact your bank or card issuer and ask about disputing the charge. Issuers set time limits for disputes, so don't let it sit for months.

:::warning[Deleting the app is not cancelling]
Removing an app from your phone doesn't cancel a subscription billed through an app store. Cancel it in your Apple or Google account's subscription settings, and keep the confirmation.
:::
