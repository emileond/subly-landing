Recurring revenue is the money clients pay you on a repeating schedule: retainers, subscriptions, maintenance plans, monthly support. It is the most predictable part of a small business's income, which makes it the part worth tracking most carefully.

Tracking it well comes down to three things. Know the monthly total (your MRR). Know when each payment is due, so you notice when one is late. And see it next to what you pay out every month, so you know what is actually left. This guide covers all three, with a worked example you can copy into a spreadsheet.

## What counts as recurring revenue?

Income counts as recurring when it repeats on a schedule and you expect it to continue without winning it again each time. For freelancers, agencies and small SaaS businesses, that usually means:

- **Client retainers**, such as a $2,500 monthly design or marketing retainer.
- **Subscriptions you sell**, monthly or yearly, for software, templates, courses or memberships.
- **Maintenance and hosting plans** you bill clients for each month.
- **Fixed monthly support or advisory fees.**

It usually does not include one-off projects, setup or onboarding fees, hourly work that varies from month to month, or a contract that has ended and has not been renewed. Those are real income, and they belong in your cash forecast, but mixing them into your recurring number makes it look steadier than it is.

:::note
Some income sits in between, like affiliate payouts that arrive every month but change in size. Track it, but keep it on a separate line so you can see how much of your income is truly fixed.
:::

## What is MRR, in plain words?

MRR, or monthly recurring revenue, is the answer to one question: if nothing changed, how much would clients pay you this month? You add up every recurring payment at its monthly value. Yearly plans are divided by 12. Payments in other currencies are converted to yours.

MRR is not the same as the money that landed in your account this month. A yearly plan arrives all at once, and a late retainer may land next month. MRR smooths that out so you can compare one month with the next.

## How do I calculate MRR? A worked example

Here is a small studio with three retainers, one of them in pounds, and a small template subscription it sells on the side. It also finished a $6,000 one-off project this month.

| Source | Billing | Amount | Monthly value (USD) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Northlight retainer | Monthly | $2,500 | $2,500.00 |
| Harbor & Co retainer | Monthly | $1,800 | $1,800.00 |
| Coastline Bakery retainer | Monthly | £1,200 | $1,560.00 |
| Templates, 40 monthly customers | Monthly | $29 each | $1,160.00 |
| Templates, 5 yearly customers | Yearly | $290 each | $120.83 |
| Website project | One-off | $6,000 | Not counted |
| **MRR** | | | **$7,140.83** |

The pound retainer is converted at an example rate of $1.30 to the pound; use the current rate when you do your own. The yearly customers are $290 × 5 ÷ 12. The project is left out because it will not repeat.

### How does MRR change from month to month?

Every change falls into one of three buckets, and it helps to track them separately:

- **New:** a new client or customer. Three new template customers add $87.
- **Expansion:** an existing client pays more. Harbor & Co moves from $1,800 to $2,200, adding $400.
- **Churn:** a client or customer leaves or pays less. One template customer cancels, removing $29.

Next month's MRR is $7,140.83 + $87 + $400 − $29 = $7,598.83. If your MRR is flat, these buckets tell you whether that is because nothing happened or because new clients are quietly replacing the ones you lose.

## How do I track client retainers and late payments?

A retainer tracker needs one row per client and a few columns:

- **Client** and the **person to contact** about invoices
- **Amount** and **currency**, exactly as billed
- **Billing schedule** (monthly, quarterly, yearly) and **due day**
- **Last payment date** and **days late**
- **Contract end or renewal date**

The column that matters most day to day is days late. A retainer that is three days late is a quick email. One that is three weeks late is a cash flow problem, especially if it is one of your largest clients.

:::steps
1. **Agree on clear terms.** Put the due date and how to pay on every invoice, and send it the day the period starts.
2. **Check due dates once a week.** Compare each retainer's due day with its last payment date. Anything past due goes on a short list.
3. **Follow up on a fixed rhythm.** For example, a friendly reminder a few days after the due date, a second note a week later, and a call after two weeks.
4. **Plan for the pattern.** If a client always pays ten days late, plan their money ten days late in your forecast rather than hoping this month will be different.
5. **Talk before it repeats.** If a client is late two months in a row, a direct conversation about timing or payment method usually helps more than more reminders.
:::

## How much is left after my tools?

MRR on its own tells you what comes in. The more useful number for a small business is what is left after the money that goes out just as regularly: software, hosting, domains, insurance, contractors on a fixed fee.

Using the studio from the example, with recurring costs of about $1,320 a month:

:::stats
- **$7,141** a month of recurring revenue
- **$1,320** a month of recurring costs
- **$5,821** a month left, before one-off work and one-off costs
:::

That remaining number is what pays for people, taxes and growth. When it shrinks, it is usually for one of two reasons: a retainer ended, or recurring costs crept up through new seats and price rises. Seeing both sides together is how you notice which. If the cost side has grown without anyone deciding it should, a [SaaS subscription audit](/blog/saas-subscription-audit) is the quickest way to bring it back. ## How do I track revenue in more than one currency?

Keep each client in the currency you bill them in. If Coastline Bakery pays £1,200, record £1,200, not the dollar amount it happened to be worth the day you set it up. Convert only when you add things up, and use a current rate.

Two practical points:

- **Your total will move even when nothing else does.** If the pound weakens, your MRR in dollars drops a little, even though the client pays exactly the same. That is worth knowing, but it is not churn.
- **What lands can differ from the conversion.** Your bank or payment provider may use its own rate and charge a fee, so the amount that arrives can be a little lower. Your statements show what really landed.
